Pronunciation

Ten consonants, five vowels, and a deliberately wide target for each. If your accent differs from mine, we can still understand each other — that is the whole design.

So if your language does not separate r from l, don't worry: in Pikotika the difference carries no meaning. A Spanish speaker, a Mandarin speaker, and a Japanese speaker saying pona will produce three audibly different words, and all three are saying it right.

Vowels are the secondary cue: nearly every word is recognizable from its consonants alone, which is why the vowel targets can be loose.

There are no diphthongs. Two vowels together are two syllables: mekaaku ‘sea’ is me-ka-A-ku, and veniire ‘trip’ is ve-ni-I-re. No single root has two vowels in a row; the pairs appear where roots meet in a compound, and in adapted names like Sutorauto (Strout), where au is two syllables.

Stress

One stress (emphasis) per word, always on the next-to-last syllable. There is nothing to memorize and no exceptions to learn.

WordStress
ponaPO-na

Click any of the words above, and then click the little play button, to hear it pronounced correctly. This works for almost any Pikotika word on this site.

A compound is one word for this purpose, however many roots it contains — kasepeste is home-animal ‘cat’, and it takes a single stress, not one per root.

A one-syllable word (for example rus) is stressed on that syllable. The three particles are the exception: ri, a and te are never stressed.

Yesterday I saw the dog.

Yanyer, eko ri vite a kanis.

Four stresses in that sentence, not six: YAN-yer, E-ko ri VI-te a KA-nis.
