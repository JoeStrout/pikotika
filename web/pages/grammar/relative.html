Relative clauses

‘The animal that the person ate.’ There are two ways to hang a clause on a noun, and one of them needs no new grammar at all.

Form one: two sentences

Say the clause as its own sentence, with ker ‘what, whichever’ filling the slot you are relativizing. Then pick it up with tis in the next clause.

PikotikaMeaning
Tu ri tene a kerkosa, pam tonar a tis ver eko.Whatever you have, send me that.
Eko ri rets veni in kerora, pam sista retover tis.Wait until I get back.

Form two: rite

Put the clause in front of the noun and close it with rite (written ⊢>).

the animal the person ate

Omo ri komi rite peste.

Send me what you have.

Pam tonar a tu ri tene rite kosa ver eko.

rite is te for clauses. Both say ‘the head follows’; the difference is how much they take.

PikotikaTakesMeaning
Omo ri komi te peste.only komiThe person is a food-animal.
Omo ri komi rite peste.the whole clausethe animal the person ate

The rule for choosing: if the modifier contains ri or a, close it with rite. Otherwise use te.

Because word order is rigid, ‘the whole clause’ is never in doubt: scanning back from rite, it takes at most one verb with its objects, ri, and one subject, stopping at the first word that would break that order.

Why you might prefer rite

It closes off the noun phrase, so other words can follow it — the ver eko in the example above. A clause sitting after a runs to the end of the sentence and cannot do that. See Subordinate clauses.
