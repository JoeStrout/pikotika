Basic sentence structure

Every Pikotika sentence has the same shape, and nothing ever moves out of it — not for a question, not for emphasis, not inside a clause buried three levels deep.

subject ri what it does or is a object
eko vite nino
Iseethe child

I see the child.

Eko ri vite a nino.

The two particles

ParticleWhat it marks
ri the end of the subject. Everything after it says something about the subject.
a the start of the object.

They are not words you translate — they are punctuation you say out loud. A listener who hears ri knows the subject just ended, however long it was, and never has to guess where one part of the sentence stops and the next begins.

ri is never dropped. If a sentence has a subject, that subject is followed by ri, every time. (A sentence with no subject at all is a different matter — see Commands and indefinite subjects.)

a appears only when there is an object. Plenty of sentences have none:

I'm going.

Eko ri ire.

Where everything else goes

A prepositional phrase normally sits at the end, after the object; one that frames the whole sentence in time or place may open it instead.

I see the fish in the water.

Eko ri vite a akupeste in aku.

Yesterday I saw the dog.

Yanyer, eko ri vite a kanis.

Modifiers — adjectives, adverbs, possessives — go immediately before the word they modify, wherever that word happens to be. They do not disturb the frame. See Modifiers.

I see a small fish.

Eko ri vite a piko akupeste.

Try it yourself

  1. Nino ri vori a aku.
    Show answer

    ‘The child wants water.’ ri closes the subject, a opens the object.

  2. Eko vemapari ri vake a pona komi.
    Show answer

    ‘My mother makes good food.’ The subject is three words long — I mother-parent — and ri is what tells you where it ends.

  3. Say ‘I see the dog’ two ways: with the dog, and without.
    Show answer

    Eko ri vite a kanis. and Eko ri vite. — drop the object and a goes with it.
