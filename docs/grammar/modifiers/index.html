Modifiers

Adjective, adverb, and possessive are one operation in Pikotika, not three: put the modifier in front of the word it modifies.

English calls itPikotikaLiterally
an adjectivetuke komisweet food
an adverbveros irefast go
a possessiveeko sanaomome heal-person

All three are the same thing: something in front, narrowing what follows. The possessor is simply a modifier — there is no my, only eko (I/me) standing where sweet stood.

Please speak slowly.

Pam nonveros tika.

We're going to my father's house.

Ekomen ri ire ver eko viropari kase.

Notice the double modifiers in eko viropari kase, my father's house.

The one exception: names

A proper noun comes after its category word, not before — omo Ar, ‘the person Hal’.

I'm going to Lisbon.

Eko ri ire ver sitas Rispan.

That is a name saying which one — which person, which city. A name used to describe rather than to identify is an ordinary modifier, and goes where every other modifier goes:

PikotikaMeaning
omo ArHal — the person of that name
Kanata omoa Canadian person

See Names and loanwords.

Stacking two or more modifiers has its own rules, and so does the boundary between the modifiers and the word they modify:
