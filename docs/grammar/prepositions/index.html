Prepositional phrases

Eleven ordinary roots do the work of English's prepositions, and the phrase they open goes at the end of the sentence.

WordSenseWordSense
inin, atsurabove, on
verto, towardtunbelow, under
vonsfromnirnear
kumwitheksoutside
porformetsbetween, among
topiabout

Where the phrase goes

At the end, after the object if there is one.

I'm going home.

Eko ri ire ver kase.

Please give that to me.

Pam tonar a tis ver eko.

The station is between the shop and the river.

Karoroko ri mets komparroko kum ronkaaku.

A phrase can be the whole thing you say

Put it straight after ri with no verb. This is how Pikotika says is here, is with me, is for you.

PikotikaLiterallyMeaning
Eko ri in tisroko.I RI in this-placeI'm here.
Eko ri kum nino.I RI with childI'm with the child.
Tis ri por tu.this RI for youThis is for you.

Two things worth knowing about kum

It is also ‘and’. Joining two like things it means and; opening a phrase of its own it means with — exactly as in English, bread and water against come with me. See And, or, but, because.

It is only ever company, never means. For a method, a manner, or a route, use in … moto (in … way):

PikotikaMeaning
in akukaro motoby ship
in Rispan motovia Lisbon
in kermotohow, by what means

How do you know Carla?

Tu ri sape a Karra in kermoto?

Try it yourself

  1. Pam veni in sens ora.
    Show answer

    ‘Please come at seven.’ in covers ‘at’ for time as well as place.

  2. Eko ri komi kum tu.
    Show answer

    ‘I'm eating with you.’ Company, so kum.

  3. How would you say ‘I'm going by car’?
    Show answer

    Eko ri ire in karo moto. — means, not company, so not kum.
