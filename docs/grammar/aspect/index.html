Time and aspect

Verbs never change form. vite is see, saw, seen and seeing; the work tenses do elsewhere is done here by ordinary words.

Time is stated once, then assumed

Say when you are talking about, and it holds until you change it. Nothing needs re-marking.

Yesterday I saw the dog.

Yanyer, eko ri vite a kanis.

Tomorrow I will see the dog.

Yantar, eko ri vite a kanis.

The verb is identical in both. If you say nothing, the listener assumes now.

Three aspect markers

Aspect — starting, finished, still going — is one word at the end.

MarkerSenseExample
apabeginningEko ri komi apa. I'm starting to eat.
vinfinishedEko ri komi vin. I have eaten.
sistastill goingEko ri komi sista. I'm still eating.

Combine a marker with a stated time and you get the rest of English's tenses for free: Yanyer, eko ri komi vin. is ‘I had eaten’.

The marker goes last, after the object

Never between the verb and a.

PikotikaMeaning
Eko ri komi a karne vin.I have eaten the meat.
Eko ri komi a karne apa.I'm starting on the meat.

It closes the whole description, not the verb — which is clearest when there is no verb at all:

I am still here.

Eko ri in tisroko sista.

A prepositional phrase may sit on either side of the marker. One that is part of what you are saying goes inside, before it; one that merely frames the sentence in time or place may follow it.

The light has been broken for months.

Vortoyan ri rompe vin in mur mese turan.

Try it yourself

  1. Musi ri apa in ots ora.
    Show answer

    ‘The music starts at eight.’ Here apa is the verb ‘begin’, not a marker — the same root does both jobs.

  2. Eko ri non pensa a ekomen ri sarve vin.
    Show answer

    ‘I don't think we've met.’ vin closes the inner clause, which is where the meeting is.

  3. Say ‘I had already finished’.
    Show answer

    Yer tempo, eko ri vin. — state the past time once, and vin does the rest.
